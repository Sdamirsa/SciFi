# Introduction

## Shape

Four to six paragraphs, 300 to 550 words for clinical journals, up to 800 for Nature-portfolio. No subheadings. In Nature-portfolio the Introduction has no heading at all and ends with a contributions list; in JAMIA, Lancet and EHJ it has the heading "Introduction" and ends with the aim.

The corpus paragraph counts: 4 (Lancet, JAMIA original, EHJ), 5 to 7 (npj, Nature Cancer, Nature Communications), 10 to 13 (Nature Article, JAMIA systematic review). The 10 to 13 paragraph versions were the hardest to read; four to six is the target.

## The funnel, paragraph by paragraph

1. **The field and the clinical stake.** Open on the clinical problem, not on AI. State the burden or the routine practice with a number and one or two citations ("a routine scan is acquired in X patients a year and used only for Y"). Ground the clinical gap in concrete figures from the literature where they exist (error rates, under-triage targets and observed rates). No "we" in this paragraph.
2. **What is currently done and why it falls short.** Current tools or prior work, their main limitation. Present tense for established facts, present perfect for the state of the literature, simple past for one named study.
3. **The specific enabling idea.** The method class (LLMs, radiomics, foundation models) in one or two sentences with its key property, glossed for a clinical reader. If the paper coins a concept (explicit versus implicit probability, a radiotranscriptomic signature), define it here once, with a short example, and use the exact term thereafter.
4. **The gap in one sentence.** "Whether X holds for Y has not been evaluated" or "existing evaluations use multiple-choice formats that do not represent Z". Contrast what prior work covers with what it does not. Avoid "to our knowledge, no study has" unless true and checked.
5. **The aim and design.** One sentence starting "We aimed to" or "In this study, we", naming the data source, the n (optional), the method, the comparator and the primary outcome. For a multicentre, prognostic or trial design, name it. Then one sentence on what the study contributes. Do not exceed 40 words in the aim sentence; the corpus has 54 and 58 word aim sentences that should have been two.

## Previewing results

- Balanced default, JAMIA, Lancet, EHJ: do not preview results in the Introduction. The hypothesis or aim closes the section.
- Nature-portfolio: end with a numbered or bulleted contributions list (two to four items), each stating what was built or found, optionally with the headline number and a pointer to the figure that shows it ("Figure 1 summarises..."). The strongest corpus example maps each bullet to one figure. Then, when Methods comes last, add one short paragraph naming the dataset, the n, the raters and the evaluation domains, and point to Methods.

## Citations

- Heavily cited in paragraphs 1 to 3 (one or two per claim, ranges for field statements), none in the aim paragraph.
- Place the citation after the specific claim, mid-sentence when one sentence carries several claims.
- Self-citation for the group's own prior work is acceptable when it is the direct antecedent, but not as the only evidence for a field claim.

## Tone

- Connectives that carry the funnel: "However" (once), "Although", "Despite", "In addition", "By contrast". Avoid opening three sentences with "Although" in one paragraph.
- No promotional framing of the method ("has revolutionised", "remarkable advances"). State what the method does and cite it.
- No regulatory or ethical commentary here; that belongs in the Discussion.

## In a review

- Paragraphs 1 to 3 as above, defining the central concept and, if new, its coined name with the acronym in parentheses.
- Paragraph 4 states the aims as a numbered list (two to four) and the scope limits (what the review excludes and why).
- Paragraph 5 is a roadmap: "We first define X. We then review Y. Finally, we propose Z." Ordinal signposts (first, then, finally) are the norm.
- A guidance paper may end with its bottom-line recommendation in one sentence; a systematic review ends with the numbered aims and names the design.
- A narrative review states in this section or at the start of the evidence section how items were found, the cut-off date, and that the search was not systematic if it was not.
