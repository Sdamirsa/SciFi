# Introduction

Registries of valvular heart disease depend on knowing how severe each lesion is, and that information sits in the free text of echocardiography reports. In most hospitals, staff abstract severity grades from these reports by hand, which delays registry entry and leaves gaps when time is short [@okafor2023registry]. Valve disease is common in older adults, so the number of reports that need abstraction grows each year [@rossi2023prevalence]. An automated method that reads existing reports could keep registries complete without adding to the workload of cardiologists and registry staff.

Rule-based systems have been the usual approach to extracting severity from echocardiography reports. They match fixed phrases and work well on the templates for which they were written, but they miss grades phrased in unexpected ways [@lindqvist2022rules]. Large language models (LLMs), which predict text from patterns learned on large corpora, can read varied phrasing without hand-written rules [@chen2024openweight]. A single-centre study found that a proprietary LLM extracted severity grades more accurately than a rule-based system from the same hospital [@mahmoudi2025echo].

Two problems stand between such results and use in a registry. Proprietary LLMs run on external servers, and many hospitals cannot send patient reports outside their network under data protection rules [@brunner2024privacy]. Open-weight models, whose parameters can be downloaded and run on hospital hardware, avoid this barrier, but their accuracy on echocardiography reports has not been measured. Clinical text models also tend to lose accuracy at hospitals other than the one where they were developed [@vandermeer2024external]. Whether an LLM run inside the hospital keeps its accuracy at a second centre is therefore not known.

We aimed to compare an open-weight LLM run on hospital hardware with a rule-based system for extracting valvular heart disease severity from 1,200 echocardiography reports at two Swiss centres, using cardiologist labels as the reference standard. The primary outcome was per-lesion accuracy at both centres combined, with accuracy at the centre not used for prompt development as the main secondary outcome. We also classified the errors that remained, because guideline grading leaves intermediate categories undefined for several lesions [@tanaka2025grading].
